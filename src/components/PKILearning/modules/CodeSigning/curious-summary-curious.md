Whenever you download an app or a software update, your computer checks a hidden digital signature. Think of this signature like a tamper-evident wax seal from the software creator (like Microsoft or Apple). If the seal is unbroken, your computer knows the update is safe to install.

However, a sufficiently capable quantum computer could threaten the public-key algorithms used by some signing systems. If an attacker could recover a trusted publisher's private key from its public key, they could create a forged signature. Whether a device accepts that software still depends on its verification policy, trust store, revocation checks, and update controls.

Post-quantum signature standards such as ML-DSA and SLH-DSA are designed to resist known quantum attacks, but deployment requires compatible signing tools, formats, trust services, and verifiers. They reduce a cryptographic risk; they do not by themselves prove that software is safe or identify who created it without a trusted certificate and policy.
