# Automotive PQC — Module Summary

## Overview

Advanced-level module (80 min, 6 workshop steps) covering post-quantum cryptography migration for the automotive industry — an ecosystem with functional safety constraints (ISO 26262), vehicle lifetimes of 15 years or more that could outlast classical signatures if a cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) arrives during a vehicle's service life (the arrival date is uncertain), complex in-vehicle network topologies, and over-the-air (OTA) update infrastructure that the vehicle must be able to trust.

**What the workshops are.** The six workshop steps are static, illustrative models built on fixed example data (sensor profiles, ECU lists, algorithm timings, protocol step sizes). They let a learner compare sizes, rates and timing budgets. They do not connect to a vehicle, an ECU or a signing service, do not configure or deploy any security control, and do not assess or certify compliance with UNECE R155/R156, ISO 26262, ISO/SAE 21434 or any other standard. Counts, timings and budgets shown in them are examples, not measurements of any real vehicle, and nothing in the module promises that a design is secure: security of a real design depends on its trust root, how keys are protected and replaced, how updates are verified, and whether the implementation is correct.

## Key Topics

- **Vehicle electrical architecture evolution**: Domain-based architecture (separate ECUs per domain: powertrain, chassis, ADAS, body, infotainment, connectivity, each with domain controller) vs. zonal architecture (physical zones with high-performance central compute nodes — HPCs — aggregating control from multiple domains). ADAS zone is the most security-critical domain in both architectures; zonal HPCs introduce new PQC upgrade points at zone boundaries.
- **In-vehicle network buses**: CAN classic (500 Kbps, 8-byte payload — no native crypto, limited to HMAC/MAC with symmetric keys via AUTOSAR SecOC), CAN-FD (up to 8 Mbps, 64-byte payload — AUTOSAR SecOC with larger MAC capacity), LIN (slave bus, up to 20 Kbps — no crypto support), FlexRay (10 Mbps, dual-channel, deterministic real-time — safety-critical), Automotive Ethernet (100BASE-T1 to 10GBASE-T1, MACsec and TLS 1.3 support — primary PQC migration target for backbone links)
- **Functional safety levels**: ISO 26262 Automotive Safety Integrity Levels ASIL A (lowest) through ASIL D (highest) and QM (non-safety). The module's examples rate braking and steering functions ASIL D; a real rating comes from the manufacturer's own safety analysis. The Safety Crypto Analyzer compares assumed ML-DSA verification times with illustrative per-function timing budgets; it does not say that ISO 26262 or any other standard requires constant-time implementations or sets those budgets. A timing budget is something the manufacturer defines and then has to measure on the target hardware. The ASIL table in the Safety Crypto Analyzer is a set of fictional example assumptions, not ISO 26262 requirements: its diagnostic-coverage column gives no percentage, because hardware metrics (ISO 26262-5, product development at the hardware level) come from the hardware safety analysis and are not cryptographic assurance. Whether a function needs a second verification path, and any dedicated hardware, comes from its own hazard analysis, safety goals and fault tolerant time interval, not from the ASIL letter; the module's classical-backup braking pattern is one example, not a requirement. LMS is a stateful scheme (a finite set of one-time keys, never reused; NIST SP 800-208), so the module treats it as a firmware-signing option and shows a symmetric MAC for real-time crash-sensor messages. ML-DSA (FIPS 204) and SLH-DSA (FIPS 205) sign by default in the hedged (randomized) variant and also permit a deterministic variant.
- **Sensor data integrity for ADAS (example profiles)**: the Sensor Data Integrity Simulator lists six example profiles — LiDAR (20 Hz point-cloud frames, ECDSA P-256 per frame in the example), radar (50 Hz, HMAC-SHA-256 per return), camera (30 Hz, ECDSA P-256 per frame "when signed"), ultrasonic and GPS/IMU (no authentication in the example) and V2X messages (10 Hz, ECDSA P-256) — each with an assumed message size and latency budget. It compares those against fixed signing and verification times for ECDSA P-256, ML-DSA-44, ML-DSA-65, FN-DSA-512 (estimated), SLH-DSA-SHA2-128s and LMS. An ML-DSA-44 signature is 2,420 bytes (FIPS 204). The figures are the module's assumptions; measure real throughput on the target hardware.
- **OTA software update architecture**: an update is only as trustworthy as the root of trust that verifies it. A layered signing chain, for example a root key kept offline in an HSM, an intermediate key used for ECU groups and a short-lived key for individual update campaigns, limits what one compromised key can sign, but it moves trust to the root key, to the code that verifies signatures in the vehicle, and to the process for replacing keys. A vehicle that can only verify RSA or ECDSA signatures chained to a root it cannot replace in the field would have no way to reject forged updates if a CRQC arrived while it was still in service; this is a forgery risk to update authenticity, not a harvest-now-decrypt-later (confidentiality) risk. Paragraph 7.2.1.1 of UN Regulation 156 requires that the authenticity and integrity of software updates be protected to reasonably prevent their compromise and reasonably prevent invalid updates; its text does not mention post-quantum cryptography. The OTA Orchestration Planner models campaign size, bandwidth, duration and ECU update order for an example fleet; it does not design a signing chain or check R156 compliance.
- **Digital car key protocols**: the module describes the Car Connectivity Consortium Digital Key 3.0 flow over NFC, BLE and UWB transports, which it states uses ECDH P-256 and ECDSA P-256 (both quantum-vulnerable). The Car Key Protocol Explorer adds up the bytes of each pairing, authentication and sharing step for a classical and an assumed post-quantum payload and flags where a transport's payload size (for example NFC short APDUs) would force fragmentation. It is an illustrative size model: it does not implement the CCC protocol, test offline operation or specify a post-quantum Digital Key.
- **Vehicle lifecycle and type approval**: UNECE R155 (cybersecurity management system, CSMS: paragraph 7.3.1 of the UN text requires the manufacturer to hold a valid Certificate of Compliance for the CSMS relevant to the vehicle type being approved, with a carve-out for type approvals before 1 July 2024; paragraph 7.3.8 requires cryptographic modules to be in line with consensus standards or the manufacturer to justify their use; the text does not mention post-quantum cryptography), UNECE R156 (software update management), ISO/SAE 21434 (road vehicles cybersecurity engineering process standard), SOTIF ISO 21448 (safety of the intended functionality). Which markets apply these UN Regulations, and from what date, is set by each jurisdiction's type-approval rules: check the type-approval authority.
- **Algorithm sizing for automotive constraints (the module's example choices, not requirements)**: ML-DSA-44 for ECU-to-ECU and OTA delta signing on capable MCUs; ML-KEM-768 for session key exchange on the Automotive Ethernet backbone; symmetric MACs (HMAC-SHA-256, AES-CMAC) kept for real-time CAN and CAN-FD buses where per-message asymmetric signatures do not fit; FN-DSA-512 as an estimated option for small ECUs. Whether a choice fits depends on measuring the target hardware and on the update, key-replacement and certification process around it.

## Workshop Steps

1. **VehicleArchitectureMapper** — Pick a vehicle type and a domain-based or zonal architecture, then select an ECU zone to read its example cryptographic requirements, quantum vulnerabilities and migration notes; summary counts (ECUs, high-risk zones, zones whose bus could carry PQC) come from fixed example data
2. **SensorDataIntegritySimulator** — Toggle example sensor profiles on and off to compare fixed signing and verification times and signature sizes of several algorithms against each sensor's assumed message rate and latency budget. It does not let the learner configure signing or MAC strategies. Includes a **V2X Channel Load** panel (model estimate): vehicles in range (10–300) × 10 Hz BSMs × signature bytes vs the 6 Mbps IEEE 802.11p default rate
3. **SafetyCryptoAnalyzer** — Select an automotive function to see its example ASIL rating, fail mode and quantum risk, a feasibility matrix of assumed verification times against example timing budgets, and an ASIL table. A panel runs functional round-trip checks of ML-DSA-44, ML-KEM-768, AES-GCM and AES-CMAC in the browser; they do not validate an automotive implementation or show compliance with the standards named beside them
4. **OTAOrchestrationPlanner** — Set an example fleet size and choose a signature algorithm to see the signature overhead, fleet bandwidth, campaign duration and critical-path time for a fixed list of ECU update targets and their dependencies. It does not design a signing chain, check R156 compliance or model the key and trust-root management behind update signing
5. **CarKeyProtocolExplorer** — Step through an illustrative Digital Key pairing, authentication and sharing flow and compare the size of a classical and an assumed post-quantum payload over NFC, BLE and UWB; it flags NFC short-APDU fragmentation. It does not run the protocol or test offline operation
6. **LifecycleMigrationRoadmap** — Enter a model year, road life, region and an assumed CRQC arrival year to see how many years of the vehicle's life would fall after that date, the example regulatory milestones for the region, HSM tier cards and lifecycle phase notes. The arrival year is an assumption set by the learner, and the output is not a migration plan or a compliance assessment

## Key Data Points

- **ECU scope (example figures)**: the module uses 100–150 ECUs for a premium vehicle and 24–30 of them as security-relevant. These are illustrative planning figures, not a universal count; ECU totals and the number of security-relevant ECUs differ by vehicle, model year and supplier architecture. The zone ECU counts in the workshop are likewise example data.
- **SAE autonomy levels**: Module uses SAE J3016 (2021). L2 (sedan), L3 conditional (SUV highway), L4 geofenced (shuttle, truck emerging). No L5 vehicles exist in production as of 2026. In the module's reading, higher autonomy adds more ECDSA-authenticated sensor and V2X paths that a CRQC could forge.
- CAN-FD maximum payload: 64 bytes — ML-DSA-44 signature (2,420 bytes) requires 38 CAN-FD frames; use AUTOSAR SecOC with AES-CMAC-128 (16 bytes) for real-time CAN buses
- Automotive Ethernet 100BASE-T1: 100 Mbps — ML-KEM-768 ciphertext (1,088 bytes) adds <0.1 ms overhead per session
- **Algorithm benchmarks** in the Sensor Data Integrity step are for ARM Cortex-A72 @ 2 GHz. Real automotive ECUs (Cortex-R52 @ 400 MHz) expect 5–20× slower. FN-DSA-512 and LMS timings are estimated from reference implementations, not validated on production automotive silicon.
- ML-DSA-44 verification time (module's model figures, not measurements): ~2–4 ms assumed on Cortex-A72; ~5–15 ms assumed on Cortex-R52 — which could exceed a 5 ms timing budget on a constrained MCU
- Signature size in a layered chain: ML-DSA-87 (4,627 bytes) + ML-DSA-65 (3,309 bytes) + ML-DSA-44 (2,420 bytes) signatures add 10,356 bytes of signature data (FIPS 204 sizes), before public keys, certificate fields and the firmware's own signature
- Service-life exposure (illustrative): a 2026 model year vehicle with an assumed 15-year road life is in service until 2041. If a CRQC arrived within that period, RSA/ECDSA signatures that the vehicle still trusts could be forged unless the verification keys and algorithms can be replaced in the field. Confidential data captured today (for example over TLS with classical key exchange) faces the separate harvest-now-decrypt-later risk
- V2X BSM: ~300-byte payload broadcast at 10 Hz per vehicle (~3 KB/s). PQC signature overhead (2,420 bytes ML-DSA-44) dwarfs the payload; constraint is signature + cert chain size, not payload budget.
- **V2X channel load (model estimate)**: BSMs at 10 Hz (SAE J2945/1) against the 6 Mbps IEEE 802.11p default data rate. Signature bytes alone fill the channel at ~30 vehicles with ML-DSA-44 (2,420 B), ~112 with FN-DSA-512 (666 B), ~1,171 with ECDSA P-256 (64 B); 100 vehicles with ML-DSA-44 = 19.36 Mbps. The model ignores the BSM payload, IEEE 1609.2 headers, periodic certificate attachment and MAC/PHY/CSMA overhead, so real congestion starts well below these counts.
- **DSRC → C-V2X**: in the US the FCC moved the 5.9 GHz ITS band from DSRC to C-V2X (ET Docket 19-138; 2020 order, finalized November 2024). 6 Mbps is the 802.11p (DSRC / ITS-G5) reference; C-V2X sidelink capacity differs, but kilobyte-scale per-message PQC signatures remain a channel-capacity problem either way.
- NFC Digital Key transactions: the Introduction treats 500 ms as the user-experience threshold for tap-and-go unlock and shows how short-APDU fragmentation of ML-KEM-768 material could exceed it; the figure is the module's planning value, so check the CCC specification for the current requirement
- UNECE R155 and R156 are UN Regulations that a market applies through its own type-approval rules, so there is no single global date and no global retroactive deadline for vehicles already on the road. R156 paragraph 3.5 requires the SUMS Certificate of Compliance to accompany the type-approval application. As an EU example, Regulation (EU) 2019/2144, Annex II item D4 (protection of the vehicle against cyberattacks), lets authorities refuse EU type-approval of new vehicle types from 6 July 2022 and prohibit registration of non-compliant new vehicles from 7 July 2024, for the vehicle categories marked in that table. For other markets, check the type-approval authority. The module does not assess any vehicle's compliance, and the workshops are learning models, not evidence of conformity
- **Regulatory confidence**: UNECE R155/R156, ISO/SAE 21434, CNSA 2.0 are published. IEEE 1609.2-2025 and ETSI TS 103 097 specify ECC (ECDSA), and no public post-quantum amendment is identified; ETSI TR 103 949 (May 2023) is a quantum-safe migration study for ITS and C-ITS. CNSA 2.0 deadlines bind National Security Systems; for other vehicles they are a reference timeline. China GB/T and Japan NISC PQC dates are projected (no official timelines published). TISAX is an information-security assessment: assessment levels AL 1-3 set how an audit provider verifies evidence and labels record the assessment objectives (for example information security or prototype protection); the ISA2027.1 catalogue (published by the VDA on 1 July 2026, for assessments ordered from 1 January 2027) has a cryptography control (5.1.1) but no post-quantum criteria, so a customer's PQC expectations are its own requirements.
- ISO/SAE 21434 TARA (Threat Analysis and Risk Assessment): The standard was published in 2021, before FIPS 203/204. In practice, an OEM might, for example, record cryptographic assets in the TARA and capture an ML-DSA/ML-KEM migration as cybersecurity goals; the standard itself does not require that migration
