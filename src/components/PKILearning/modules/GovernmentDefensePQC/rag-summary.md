# Government & Defense PQC

## Overview

The Government & Defense module teaches the federal post-quantum policy layer — the instruments that turn post-quantum cryptography from an engineering preference into a procurement requirement. It covers the CNSA 2.0 suite and precisely what it replaces from CNSA 1.0, the dated CNSSP 15 milestones that bind National Security Systems, the National Security System versus federal civilian distinction that decides which deadline applies to a given system, the Federal PKI certificate profile pair (one published and classical, one draft and post-quantum), and how the requirement propagates outward through procurement to contractors who never touch an NSS. It carries genuine dated deadlines rather than advisory guidance: the CNSSP 15 dates for National Security Systems and, for federal civilian high value assets and high impact systems, the Executive Order 14412 dates.

## Key Concepts

- **CNSA 2.0 replaces every public-key line of CNSA 1.0 and almost nothing else.** Key establishment moves from ECDH P-384 / RSA-3072 to ML-KEM-1024 (FIPS 203); general-purpose signatures move from ECDSA P-384 / RSA-3072 to ML-DSA-87 (FIPS 204); software and firmware signing moves to LMS or XMSS (NIST SP 800-208), with ML-DSA-87 also approved for it. AES-256 is unchanged and SHA-384 becomes SHA-384 or SHA-512. That asymmetry is the central budgeting lesson: "replace all cryptography" is the wrong model, because the symmetric half of the estate survives the transition intact
- **Level-5 parameters only.** CNSA 2.0 approves ML-KEM-1024 and ML-DSA-87 exclusively. ML-KEM-512, ML-KEM-768, ML-DSA-44 and ML-DSA-65 are FIPS-approved but not CNSA 2.0-approved — an NSS-specific narrowing of the NIST standards, not a restatement of them
- **Firmware signing moved first, and NSA said why.** Three stated reasons: NIST had already standardised the algorithms, the use case is more urgent than others, and stateful hash-based signatures have the longest cryptanalytic history — so their performance costs matter least here. LMS and XMSS are the approved SP 800-208 schemes (all parameter sets; NSA recommends LMS with SHA-256/192) and the multi-tree variants HSS and XMSS^MT are not allowed. Validated ML-DSA-87 is also approved for software and firmware signing, but NSA wants LMS and XMSS adopted now where possible because validated implementations have been available longer; ML-DSA can make sense when a signing strategy needs more signatures than a single LMS or XMSS key can reasonably provide, or in distributed signing environments
- **The dated CNSSP 15 milestones** (as stated in the CNSA 2.0 FAQ Ver 2.2, October 2026):
  - **1 January 2027** — CNSA 2.0 algorithms will be required in all new acquisitions for NSS
  - **31 December 2030** — all NSS equipment and services that cannot support CNSA 2.0 must be phased out unless otherwise granted an exception; NSA also expects encryption to be broadly quantum resistant by default by this date
  - **31 December 2031** — CNSA 2.0 algorithms are mandated for use, unless otherwise noted, to achieve full quantum resistance for NSS
  - **2035** — the goal set in NSM-10 for achieving quantum resistance; NSA intends that all NSS will be quantum-resistant well ahead of 2035
- **The advisory and the FAQ state different dates.** The September 2022 advisory carried a per-equipment-type timetable running from 2025 to 2033; the CNSA 2.0 FAQ (Ver 2.2, October 2026) says that the update to CNSSP 15 set several dates and states them as listed above. The FAQ does not restate the advisory's 2033 date
- **NSA does not require hybrids for NSS, and that is not a prohibition.** The FAQ states NSA "has confidence in CNSA 2.0 algorithms and will not require NSS developers to use hybrid certified products for security purposes," while explicitly allowing that "product availability and interoperability requirements may lead to adopting hybrid solutions." This is routinely misreported as a ban. NSA also acknowledges some protocols may need hybrid-like constructions simply to carry the larger ML-KEM-1024 and ML-DSA-87 objects
- **NSA's four stated objections to hybrids**: added protocol and library complexity (extra negotiation, error handling, modified APIs, more testing); interoperability becomes harder because every party must share both component algorithms _and_ the hybridisation method; it buys a second transition later when classical components are eventually dropped; and more security products fail from implementation flaws than from cryptanalysis, so added complexity is itself a risk. These are the strongest published arguments against the hybrid-by-default posture most of the commercial world has adopted — and the opposite of what ETSI specifies for European trust services
- **Naming drift is an inventory problem.** The September 2022 advisory names the algorithms CRYSTALS-Kyber and CRYSTALS-Dilithium because FIPS 203/204 did not exist yet; the October 2026 FAQ (Ver 2.2) calls the same algorithms ML-KEM-1024 and ML-DSA-87. The two documents name the same algorithms differently. Estate inventory tooling must search both vocabularies, because a search for "Kyber" will not find documents written after standardisation
- **NSS and federal civilian are governed by different instruments.** CNSSP 15 carries the CNSA 2.0 dates and binds National Security Systems. Federal civilian agencies follow the Quantum Computing Cybersecurity Preparedness Act, OMB M-23-02 / M-26-15 and Executive Order 14412 (22 June 2026): inventory, reporting and migration plans, plus **dated PQC deadlines for high value assets and high impact systems** — key establishment by 31 December 2030 and digital signatures by 31 December 2031 — which exclude National Security Systems, whose CNSSP 15 requirements and timelines NSA describes as comparable. Which stack applies to a system is the first question to answer, because it determines which dates apply
- **The federal mandate stack**:
  - **Quantum Computing Cybersecurity Preparedness Act (2022)** — Law. Federal civilian executive-branch agencies. Requires inventory of CRQC-vulnerable cryptographic systems and migration, with OMB reporting. The statute is what makes the rest of the stack enforceable rather than advisory
  - **OMB M-23-02** — To comply with NSM-10, directs agencies to submit a prioritised inventory of CRQC-vulnerable cryptographic systems (excluding National Security Systems) by 4 May 2023 and annually thereafter until 2035, or as directed by superseding guidance
  - **OMB M-26-15** — Implements the accelerated migration set by Executive Order 14412 (22 June 2026), and fulfils OMB's responsibility under the QCCPA to issue guidance directing each agency to prioritise critical IT for migration to PQC and to plan that migration. Each agency submits a PQC migration plan within 120 days, phased from discovery (2026–2027) through pilots (2027–2028), prioritised key-establishment migration (2028–2030) and signature migration (2031) to full migration (2035). It does not apply to National Security Systems
  - **Executive Order 14306** — Sustains and amends prior federal cybersecurity direction, including the PQC provisions driving the CISA product-category work
  - **Executive Order 14412 (22 June 2026)** — Section 4(b) directs OMB to require agencies to move all high value assets and high impact systems (not National Security Systems) to PQC for key establishment by 31 December 2030 and for digital signatures by 31 December 2031. Section 6(c) directs the FAR Council to publish, within 180 days, a proposed rule requiring covered contractors to comply by 31 December 2030 with NIST FIPS, including all applicable FIPS incorporating PQC-compliant algorithms; the order directs a proposed rule, it is not itself that rule
  - **CNSS Policy 15** — The instrument that actually carries the CNSA 2.0 dates, for National Security Systems
  - **NIST SP 800-171 Rev. 3** — Reaches nonfederal systems processing Controlled Unclassified Information: contractors, universities and suppliers who never touch an NSS. NIST states that it supersedes Rev. 2 (February 2020), but DoD's CMMC briefing (cleared for open publication 21 January 2025) says CMMC assessments remain against Rev. 2 until DoD formally adopts Rev. 3 through rulemaking
- **Federal PKI has two certificate profiles, and only one is binding.** The Common Policy X.509 Certificate and CRL Profile is published, in force, and classical (RSA, SHA). A separate PQC profile dated **21 April 2026** is a **DRAFT**, adding ML-DSA and ML-KEM for CITE testing starting from v2.2 of the Common Policy profiles. A draft is not contractually bindable — teaching the pair side by side is crypto agility as an actual document lifecycle rather than a slogan. Separately, the Federal PKI page announced that the Federal Bridge Certification Authority (FBCA) G4 would be replaced by the FBCA G5 starting 27 August 2026 to meet federal cryptographic-strength requirements (applications may need new intermediate CA certificates in their trust stores), and under the Common Policy CA private keys must be held in cryptographic modules validated to at least FIPS 140 Level 3
- **The 1 January 2027 date is a requirement with exceptions, not an automatic ban.** CNSA 2.0 algorithms will be required in all new NSS acquisitions; the FAQ (version 2.2, October 2026) says that where no CNSA 2.0 compliant product meeting the need is available on 1 January 2027, a deployment may, if needed, be accepted on a vendor's contractual commitment to provide a CNSA 2.0 update, combined with a realistic deployment plan that prioritises partial quantum resistance and meets the 2030 deadline for full quantum resistance, and NIAP Policy Letter #33 (effective 1 January 2027) says government contracts must include language requiring products used in NSS to implement PQC or be updateable to implement PQC
- **The CSfC PQC Guidance Addendum is a draft.** Version 1.0 draft .5 (4 April 2025) sets out a planned timeline, subject to change, that aims for all CSfC solutions to implement post-quantum cryptography by 2030. NSA's CNSA 2.0 FAQ (version 2.2, October 2026) says NIAP will begin archiving the certificates of partially quantum-resistant and quantum-vulnerable products generally within a year of fully quantum-resistant products coming on the market, that it expects this archiving to start in late 2027, and that products with archived certificates will not comply with CNSSP 11 and will not be appropriate for NSS
- **Algorithm support is not the only procurement gate.** CNSSP 11 requires commercial IA and IA-enabled IT products bought to protect information on NSS to comply with the NIAP program (agencies select evaluated products from the NIAP Product Compliant List) and, where applicable, the FIPS cryptographic validation programs; the CNSA 2.0 FAQ says NIAP and FIPS validation are required in most circumstances
- **The CISA PQC product category list is broader than CNSA 2.0.** It names ML-KEM (FIPS 203), ML-DSA (FIPS 204), SLH-DSA (FIPS 205) and the stateful hash-based signatures of SP 800-208, and says organizations should acquire only PQC-capable products in the categories it lists as widely available; NSA does not approve SLH-DSA, HSS or XMSS^MT for National Security Systems

## Workshop / Interactive Activities

The workshop has 3 hands-on tools:

1. **CNSA 1.0 → 2.0 Suite Comparator** — which suite lines are replaced and which survive, with NSA's own stated rationale per line. Makes the "symmetric primitives survive" point concrete rather than asserted
2. **Federal Mandate Explorer** — pick a system type and see which instruments bind it, and critically, whether it has a dated deadline at all
3. **Federal PKI Profile Pair** — the classical certificate profile beside the draft PQC profile, and why a draft cannot be cited as binding

## Related Standards

CNSSP 15 carries the dates for National Security Systems. The QCCPA and OMB M-23-02 / M-26-15 carry inventory, reporting and migration-plan obligations for federal civilian systems, and Executive Order 14412 sets dated PQC deadlines for their high value assets and high impact systems (key establishment by 31 December 2030, digital signatures by 31 December 2031). NIST SP 800-171 Rev. 3 reaches nonfederal systems handling Controlled Unclassified Information (DoD's CMMC briefing, cleared for open publication 21 January 2025, says CMMC assessments remain against Rev. 2 until DoD formally adopts Rev. 3 through rulemaking), and Executive Order 14412 directs a proposed FAR rule requiring covered contractors to comply with NIST FIPS, including all applicable FIPS incorporating PQC-compliant algorithms, by 31 December 2030. CNSSP 11 requires commercial IA and IA-enabled IT products bought for National Security Systems to comply with the NIAP program and, where applicable, the FIPS cryptographic validation programs, and NIAP Policy Letter #33 sets the timeline for post-quantum requirements in NIAP evaluations. The Federal PKI Common Policy certificate profile is in force and classical; a draft profile dated 21 April 2026 adds ML-DSA and ML-KEM for CITE testing. Underlying algorithm standards are FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA, named by the CISA list but not approved for NSS) and NIST SP 800-208 (LMS/XMSS). RFC 8603 profiles the earlier CNSA suite for X.509. Related guidance includes the NSA CSfC PQC Guidance Addendum (draft), the CISA PQC product category list (2026) and the CMMC 2.0 model.

Cross-reference: the **Hybrid Cryptography** module reaches a different default — hybrid recommended or mandated — and the **Trust Services** module covers ETSI TS 119 312 V2.1.1, which _requires_ hybrid signatures for European qualified signatures. Same algorithms, different authorities, different defaults. Which applies is a jurisdiction question, not a cryptography question.
