# OT & Industrial Control Systems PQC — Module Summary

## Overview

Advanced module (90 min, 10 learn sections, 6 workshop steps) on post-quantum cryptography for operational technology (OT) in five sectors: energy, water and wastewater, rail and transit, manufacturing and process, and building automation. OT ranks safety first, then availability, integrity and confidentiality. The main quantum threat in OT is authenticity — a cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) could forge the RSA/ECDSA signatures that controllers use to accept firmware, project downloads, certificates and commands — rather than harvest-now-decrypt-later (HNDL), which matters mostly at remote-access, IIoT and inter-site boundaries. OT is reached along three paths: IT-to-OT lateral movement, remote access and connected devices ending at L3.5, and physical or local access at L1/L0. Assets can stay in service for decades (NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3: the lifespan of an OT system can exceed 20 years), and changing crypto in a safety-related device can mean re-testing a protection scheme, a SIL assessment or a rail safety case. Smart meters, AMI and RF mesh are covered in the IoT module (`iot-pqc`).

## Learn sections

- **Why OT is different** (`why-ot`): safety > availability > integrity > confidentiality; forgery vs HNDL; long lifecycles (NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3: the lifespan of an OT system can exceed 20 years; the Applied Quantum PQC Migration Framework: OT systems (SCADA, PLCs, RTUs) often have 15–25 year lifecycles). The module's typical lives are its own illustrative figures, not values from a standard: SCADA/HMI servers 7–15 years, relays, PLCs and safety instrumented systems 15–25, RTUs 15–20, rail signalling 25–40, substation primary plant 30–50; recertification cost.
- **Purdue, zones and conduits** (`architecture`): Purdue levels L0–L5 grouped into IEC 62443 zones and conduits; SL-T (target), SL-C (capability), SL-A (achieved). CR 4.3 (IEC 62443-4-2), 'Use of cryptography', is quoted by Siemens (Fries, ITU-T X.509 Day, September 2025) as requiring cryptographic security mechanisms according to internationally recognized and proven security practices and recommendations; that wording names no algorithm or PQC. IEC 62443-3-3 (SR 4.3) is not among the documents the module holds, so its wording is not described.
- **OT attack surface: three paths in** (`attack-surface`): (A) IT-to-OT lateral movement, L4/5 → L3.5 → L3 → L2: enterprise identity, PKI and Active Directory trust vouch for every hop, so migrating the IT side is part of protecting OT (CISA PQC considerations for OT; NIST SP 800-82r3 asks for separate OT accounts and a monitored DMZ; CISA/FBI/EPA/DOE Primary Mitigations 2025; CISA AA21-131A, where ransomware on a pipeline operator's IT network led it to disconnect OT systems). (B) Remote access and connected devices ending at L3.5: vendor VPNs, jump hosts and IIoT platforms; NIST SP 800-82r3 §5.4.2 routes the IIoT platform tier through the DMZ border firewall, and a direct cloud link from L1–L2 is a DMZ bypass (§5.3.7); hybrid ML-KEM on the link is the first PQC step. (C) Physical or local access at L1/L0 (sometimes called close access): CISA concludes that secure versions of industrial protocols remain underutilized in OT environments, and its 2026 study "Barriers to Secure OT Communication" found many systems built today still trust anyone with access, and SP 800-82r3 §5.3.6 says many Level 0 devices cannot be authenticated; that is a present-day gap fixed with classical authentication or a gateway, and PQC goes into firmware signing and key distribution. Worked example: Ukraine, December 2015 (CISA IR-ALERT-H-16-056-01), which used all three paths.
- **OT protocol native security** (`ot-protocols`): IEC 62351-6 (2020) protects GOOSE and Sampled Values with profiles that include HMAC-SHA256 and AES-GMAC; group keys come from a GDOI key distribution centre (IEC 62351-9, RFC 8052), not RSA certificates. MMS uses IEC 62351-3 TLS; IEC 62351-3:2023 adds a TLS 1.3 profile and now lists the mandatory TLS 1.2 cipher suites itself; it profiles conventional TLS cipher suites and does not specify PQC key-exchange or signature algorithms. An industry presentation (Siemens, ITU-T X.509 Day, September 2025) reports that the PQC transition for IEC 62351 has started in IEC TC57, with further refinements needed, and cites IEC 62351-90-4 for guidance on migrating from one cryptographic algorithm to another. DNP3 SAv5: HMAC challenge-response, AES key wrap of session keys, and pre-shared update keys by default; where they are changed remotely, the 2011 IEEE 1815 working draft makes the symmetric method (AES-256 key wrap plus HMAC-SHA-256) the default and the asymmetric methods, which use RSA and DSA, optional. IEEE SA lists IEEE 1815-2012 as an Inactive-Reserved standard (inactivated 30 March 2023) and lists a P1815 project that would supersede it; the documents the module holds describe no revision named "SAv6". IEC 60870-5-104 uses IEC 62351-3 TLS and IEC 62351-5 authentication. OPC UA SecurityPolicies use RSA or ECC. CIP Security (EtherNet/IP) uses TLS/DTLS with certificates or PSKs across five profiles. PROFINET Security Classes 1–3. Modbus/TCP Security wraps Modbus in mutual TLS; plain Modbus has none. BACnet/SC uses TLS 1.3 with elliptic-curve crypto only. DLMS/COSEM suites 0/1/2 use AES-GCM, ECDH/ECDSA P-256 or P-384 — no RSA. IEC 62351 part scopes: Part 3 TLS, Part 4 MMS, Part 5 IEC 60870-5 and DNP3, Part 6 IEC 61850, Part 8 RBAC, Part 9 key management, Part 14 security event logging (syslog/TLS, so quantum-exposed).
- **Safety-critical timing** (`safety-timing`): IEC 61850-5 puts GOOSE trip messages in transfer-time class TT6 (3 ms or less). IEC 61869-9 SV rates include 4,000, 4,800 and 14,400 samples/s (250, 208 and 69 µs apart). PQC stays out of the trip path; the symmetric MAC is already quantum-safe. PTP security is IEEE 1588-2019 Annex P (not NTS). IEC 61511 Ed.2 clause 8.2.4 requires a security risk assessment that includes the SIS (per Derbyshire; the standard text is not held).
- **Firmware and project signing** (`firmware-project-signing`): vendor firmware and engineering-workstation project signing; NERC CIP-010-4 R1.6 (software source and integrity verification — not CIP-007); IEC 62443-4-1 and 4-2 CR 3.4; CNSA 2.0 lists LMS and XMSS (SP 800-208) for software/firmware signing and ML-DSA-87 as its signature parameter set; prefer from 2025, exclusive by 2030. LMS is stateful (counter must never repeat; SP 800-208 requires hardware modules that prevent state cloning); ML-DSA is stateless.
- **Remote access and boundaries** (`remote-access`): industrial DMZ, jump hosts, IIoT platforms on their own segment routed through the DMZ, data diodes (no key exchange to break, but passed files still need signature checks), NERC CIP-005-7 R2 (Interactive Remote Access through an intermediate system with encryption and MFA) and R3 (vendor remote access for EACMS and PACS). For IPsec/IKEv2 VPNs: RFC 8784 (Standards Track) stirs an additional shared secret into the IKEv2 key derivation, so that, where the two ends share a long secret key, the exchange is quantum secure without waiting for new key-exchange algorithms; RFC 9370 lets IKEv2 add a post-quantum key exchange alongside the classical (EC)DH one; as of 21 August 2026 (version 12), post-quantum signature authentication for IKEv2 was specified in an IETF Internet-Draft, draft-ietf-ipsecme-ikev2-pqc-auth.
- **Sector deep dives** (`sectors`): Energy (substations; NERC CIP-012-2 effective 2026-07-01 adds availability and is technology-neutral; TSA SD Pipeline-2021-02G for TSA-designated pipelines; for IEEE 2030.5 distributed energy resources the SunSpec/Sandia workgroup recommends TLS 1.3 for all DER communications (SAND2022-1118), and a 2025 conference abstract reports a hybrid quantum-safe prototype of OCPP for EV charging that names no algorithm). Water (EPA's 2023 sanitary-survey cybersecurity move was withdrawn the same year; AWIA §2013 risk assessments and voluntary EPA/AWWA guidance remain). Rail (UNISIG SUBSET-137 KMC names RSA and ECDH; FRMCS / ETSI TS 103 764 names no algorithm — its security is layered, with a TLS-backed, mutually authenticated Local Binding between applications and the On-Board FRMCS or Trackside Gateway, and the transport and service layers defer to 3GPP security specifications; 49 CFR Part 236 Subpart I names no algorithm). Manufacturing and process (PLC/DCS, OPC UA, CIP Security, PROFINET, IEC 61511 SIS). Building automation (BACnet/SC).
- **Brownfield retrofit** (`retrofit`): firmware update, VPN overlay, bump-in-the-wire / inline appliance, replacement. Overlays protect the wire, not the device's own firmware trust.
- **Key regulations** (`regulations`): IEC 62443; NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3 (no mention of quantum computing); CISA PQC considerations for OT; NIS2 Art. 21(2)(h) and the EU coordinated PQC roadmap (start by end of 2026, high-risk use cases by end of 2030, as much as feasible by 2035); NERC CIP-002, CIP-005-7, CIP-010-4, CIP-012-2, CIP-013-2, CIP-015-1; TSA SD Pipeline-2021-02G (3 May 2026 – 2 May 2027; operational-technology services may not traverse the IT system unless their content is encrypted while in transit); CNSA 2.0's earlier category preferences (firmware signing 2025, networking equipment 2026) plus the current FAQ version 2.2 milestones (new NSS acquisitions from 2027, unsupported equipment and services phased out by the end of 2030, CNSA 2.0 algorithms mandated by the end of 2031 unless otherwise noted). The separate 2035 date is the broader NSM-10 quantum-risk objective, not the current CNSA 2.0 algorithm mandate. CNSA 2.0 does not generally require hybrids, and AES-256 has no stated sunset.

## Workshop steps

1. **Protocol Analyzer** (`protocol-security-analyzer`): 14 OT protocols (IEC 61850 GOOSE, SV and MMS; DNP3 SAv5; IEC 60870-5-104; ICCP/TASE.2; OPC UA; CIP Security; PROFINET Security Classes; Modbus/TCP Security; BACnet/SC; DLMS/COSEM; IEEE 2030.5; IEEE 1588 PTP), each split into crypto layers tagged forgery, HNDL, both, symmetric (quantum-safe) or no crypto, with a sector filter and FIPS 203/204 size deltas. A KAT panel checks HMAC-SHA-256 against a NIST ACVP HMAC-SHA2-256 sample (FIPS 198-1) and AES-256 key wrap against RFC 3394 §4.6.
2. **Zone & Conduit Planner** (`zone-conduit-planner`): nine IEC 62443 zones on the Purdue model, including IIoT & cloud connectors as a separate L3.5 segment. Each zone has three settings: firmware and project signing, command authentication, and traffic protection. Forgery uses the stronger of the two authentication settings, HNDL is scored separately, and the zone is ranked by the larger; an unauthenticated setting is flagged as a present-day gap, not scored as quantum risk. L1, SIS and L0 default to unauthenticated commands with classically signed firmware. Defaults: IIoT & cloud connectors, Remote & vendor access and Industrial DMZ 100 (HNDL), Basic control and SIS 90 (forgery), Process / field devices 80, Enterprise 70, Supervisory control 64, Site operations 50. Ten conduits with PQC actions, each tagged with its attack path (A IT-to-OT, B remote access and connected devices, C physical or local access) and filterable by path; the IIoT-to-site-operations conduit is marked as a DMZ bypass. Model estimate.
3. **Substation Planner** (`substation-migration-planner`): six substation zones (WAN / control-centre links, station bus MMS, engineering access, IED firmware signing, process bus GOOSE/SV, PTP time sync) scored by forgery and HNDL exposure weighted by NERC CIP impact; complexity changes effort only. A high-impact transmission substation on fibre with 80 IEDs ranks WAN 91, station bus 88, engineering 87, IED firmware 70, process bus 34, time sync 21, with a 3 ms (TT6) trip budget. Model estimate.
4. **Safety & Consequence Scorer** (`safety-consequence-scorer`): eight scenarios across five sectors, scored on consequence severity, people affected, command and firmware authentication, independent safety layer (IEC 61511) and asset life. Defaults: hydro spillway 90, rail ETCS 72, chemical SIS 63 (critical); water treatment 59, gas pipeline 46, transmission SCADA 44 (high); hospital building automation 33 (medium); assembly line 14 (low). With PQC firmware signing the gas pipeline drops to 9. Model estimate, not a SIL determination.
5. **Sector Roadmap** (`sector-migration-roadmap`): six phases (inventory, boundary, signing roots, supervisory, site rollout, legacy) driven by sector, programme size, budget pace, jurisdiction, site count and CRQC planning year. Defaults (energy, medium programme, 50 sites, US, 2033): 60 months, ending 2030, signing roots done 2027. At 400 sites the rollout runs 192 months and the programme ends in 2043. No costs are shown. Model estimate.
6. **Firmware & Project Signing Lab** (`firmware-project-signing-lab`): LMS H10–H25 and HSS L=2 (sizes computed from RFC 8554; H20/W8 = 1,776 B signature, 60 B public key), XMSS (size only) and ML-DSA-65 / ML-DSA-87 (FIPS 204; ML-DSA-87 = 4,627 B). Shows signatures per HSM, years until the key is exhausted and state-management warnings; an LMS H10 tree used for project signing at 5,000 signatures a year is exhausted after 0.2 years. ML-DSA is checked by NIST ACVP ML-DSA sigVer KATs; LMS and XMSS rows are labelled simulated (no signature computed).
