# Custody Governance & Assurance — In Simple Terms

## What This Is About

Buying an HSM, holding a SOC 2 report, or showing a crime-insurance certificate does not automatically prove a digital-asset custodian can stop unauthorized transfers. This module explains how to test a custody architecture with six practical evaluation questions, how major jurisdictions actually regulate key management, and what insurance, audits, and proof-of-reserves reports do and do not prove.

## Why It Matters

Governance teams often face bold vendor claims ("policy enforced in hardware", "fully insured", "audited proof of reserves") and overlapping regulatory acronyms across the EU, Hong Kong, Dubai, New York, Singapore, Japan, and Switzerland. Without separating binding statutes from supervisory guidance, accounting rules, and broker-reported market practice, decision-makers can miss real signing-authority gaps or rely on reports whose scope never tested key management.

## The Key Takeaway

Judge custody governance by three tests: (1) where transaction parsing, policy checks, and recovery authority actually sit under explicit Rule G1 assumptions; (2) which regulatory texts legally apply to your entity and licence — quoted verbatim or explicitly marked "not stated"; and (3) whether an audit or insurance policy actually covers the signing boundary rather than merely reporting on a narrow perimeter or capping financial loss after a breach.

## What's Happening

In the interactive Regulation & Assurance Control Mapper (W11), you pick a built-in custody architecture preset, apply applicability gates (entity type, licence, binding force), and compare two or more jurisdictions side by side alongside market-practice insurer questions and auditor tests — with US federal accounting and permissibility texts separated into their own context band and no fabricated compliance verdicts.
